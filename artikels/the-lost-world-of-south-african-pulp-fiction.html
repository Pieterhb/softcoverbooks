The Lost World of South African Pulp Fiction: Reviving the "Sakboekie"

From the "No TV" era of the 1950s to the global stage: The history of the Big 9 series and the mission to save them.

The reading bug didn’t just bite me when I was a young boy in primary school. It devoured me. By the time I reached high school, I was hopelessly addicted to a good story. I didn’t know it then, but that childhood obsession would eventually define my adult life, turning a hobby into a full-time mission to save a piece of literary history.

About 30 years ago, looking at the mountain of softcover books I had collected over the years, I made a decision. I wanted to do two things. Deep-dive into the history of South Africa’s unique pulp fiction industry, and digitally preserve the cover art of these nostalgic treasures for the world to see.

I started by simply scanning covers for my website, Softcoverbooks.co.za, purely for nostalgic reasons. But one thing led to another. Min het ek geweet (Little did I know) where that hobby would lead me. From simply buying and enjoying books, to building a cover-art database, to writing my own stories, and finally, to my current path. Translating these legendary South African adventures into English.

My goal? To introduce the world to literary giants like F.A. Venter, Gerrie Radlof, and Braam le Roux.

But to understand why this matters, we have to go back to a very specific, and very strange time in history.

The “No TV” Phenomenon: Why South Africa Read Like Crazy (1950–1976)

After World War II, the whole world saw a boom in reading. People were hungry for escapism. However, in the USA and UK, the golden age of pulp fiction began to die out in the 1950s as television took over.

South Africa was different. We didn’t get television until 1976.

For 26 years, while the rest of the world was gluing themselves to screens, South Africa’s primary form of evening entertainment remained the written word. The demand for cheap, episodic, action-packed stories was insatiable. It filled the void that Netflix or TV series fill today.

This created a unique industry: the “Sakboekie” (Pocketbook).

These weren’t just sold in bookstores. You bought them at the local “Greek Café” on the corner, at newsstands, and in supermarkets like OK Bazaars along with your bread and milk. In the 1950s and 60s, the average Afrikaans household consumed 3 to 5 of these booklets a week. It was an era of mass-market penetration that has arguably never been beaten since.

The Second Wind: The Sirkel & Vanity Era (Late 70s – Early 90s)

While the original craze began in the 50s, my personal nostalgia, and the focus of my project, lies in the revival era of the late 1970s through to the early 1990s.

During this time, two key publishers, Sirkel Publikasies and later Vanity Promosies, undertook a massive project to reprint the cream of the crop. They brought the “Golden Age” back to life for a new generation.

These reprints were iconic, largely thanks to their artwork. The vibrant, action-packed covers (some even extending onto the back cover) were initially painted by Chris du Plooy, with Willie du Plooy taking over later. I have never been able to confirm if they were brothers, family, or if the shared surname was pure coincidence, but their brushstrokes defined the imagination of a generation.

The “Big 9”: A Deep Dive into the Legends

My research identified over 60 different pulp series from the grand era of South African fiction. However, Sirkel and Vanity focused on the absolute best. The “Big 9” Super Series. My ultimate goal is translating this Big 9 series for a global audience, alongside select other series..

Here is the breakdown of the giants I am working to revive:

1. The Action & Adventure Heavyweights

  • Sahara-Avontuur (40 Books): The French Foreign Legion Saga
    Currently my main focus, this series follows Teuns Stegmann, a South African soldier in the French Foreign Legion. Written by F.A. Venter (under the pseudonym Meiring Fouché), it is pure, high-octane pulp. Think cursed rubies, radioactive mines, and mad scientists in the deep desert.

  • Die Swart Luiperd (70 Books): The Jungle Justice
    Perhaps the most famous series of all. The hero, Leon Fouché (known as The Black Leopard), fights crime in the African jungles accompanied by his two tame leopards, Simson and Spikkels. It was originally started by Braam le Roux, but after his tragic early death, F.A. Venter took over to keep the legend alive.

  • Temmers van die Woestyn (74 Books): Gritty Survival
    Often compared to Sahara-Avontuur, this series is darker and grittier. Written by Braam le Roux and later Reinhardt Hendriks, it focuses less on military glory and more on the brutal reality of survival and warfare in the unforgiving desert sands.

  • Rooi Jan (40 Books): The “Boer Tarzan”
    Set during the Great Trek, this series by Casper H. Marais features a muscular hero named Rooi Jan, his horse Blits, and his dog Leeu. It captured the imagination of young boys who dreamed of the frontier life, blending history with wild adventure.

  • Ramala (32 Books): The Masked Avenger
    Set during the Eastern Cape frontier wars of the 1840s, this series features Manie van der Walt, a farmer who dons a mask to become “Ramala”. A Zorro-like figure fighting for justice. Written by the prolific Gerrie Radlof, it is a masterclass in suspense.

2. The Sea & Detective Classics

  • Oloff die Seerower (24 Books): High Seas Rebellion
    Written by Gerrie Radlof (pseudonym for Gerrit Lubbe van Zyl), this series tells the story of Oloff van Wagenaar. Falsely accused of murder, Oloff flees to the sea, becoming a “noble pirate” to clear his name and fight real criminals.

  • Maagd van die See (9 Books): Nautical Mystery
    A shorter, cult-classic series by Meiring Fouché featuring Captain Simon Verbeeck. It involves lost treasures and mysteries in the West Indies. A rare departure from the African setting.

  • Die S.A. Polisie-reeks & Swerwer-Speurder: Crime & Punishment
    These two series (totaling 46 books) brought the “hardboiled” detective genre to South Africa. Featuring detectives like Kobus Roode and the private eye Obed de Swardt, they offered a local twist on the American crime thriller.

A Collector’s Guide: What to Look For

If you are new to the world of South African “Sakboekies” or are browsing antique shops, knowing what you are looking at can be tricky. Over the last 30 years of scanning covers for Softcoverbooks.co.za, here are the three things I tell every new collector to watch out for:

1. The “Pronk” vs. “Sirkel” Editions
The original 1950s prints (often published by Pronkboeke) are the “Holy Grail” for purists. However, they were printed on very cheap, acidic post-war paper, meaning they are often extremely fragile today. The 1980s Sirkel/Vanity reprints are much sturdier and feature the iconic, vibrant Du Plooy cover art. For most collectors, the 80s reprints are the best entry point.

2. The Numbering Chaos
One of the biggest headaches for collectors is the numbering. Publishers in the 70s and 80s were notorious for printing books out of order. For example, the Ramala reprints by Sirkel mixed up the original storyline sequence. If you are trying to read them chronologically, check the database on my website first!

3. The “Lost” Authors
Many fans don’t realize that Braam le Roux (creator of Die Swart Luiperd) died at the young age of 38. Had he lived, he might have become the single greatest Afrikaans adventure writer in history. Collecting his original works is not just a hobby. It is a tribute to a massive talent gone too soon.

The Road Ahead: Preserving the Legacy

This is a massive undertaking. Of the 335 books in these top 9 series, I have successfully republished over 100 of them in Afrikaans and English.

The journey isn’t without its hurdles. Some series, such as Rooi Jan and Ramala, contain themes rooted in the political and social mindset of the 1950s that are sensitive today. To release them to a modern global audience, they require significant rewriting and adaptation to ensure they are appropriate while keeping the spirit of the adventure alive. It is a pity, as these were some of the most popular stories of their time, but I am determined to find a way to save them.

For now, the work continues. From scanning covers 30 years ago to publishing eBooks for the world today, I am driven by one thing. Ensuring these stories don’t disappear into the dust of history, but live on forever.
